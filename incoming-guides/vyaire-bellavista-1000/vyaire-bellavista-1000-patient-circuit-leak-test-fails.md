## What This Guide Helps With

Troubleshoot circuit leak-test failures caused by loose connections, damaged tubing, accessories, humidification components, sensors, valves, or incorrect circuit assembly.

## Step-by-Step Troubleshooting

### 1. Protect the Patient and Use an Alternate Ventilator

Do not troubleshoot a circuit that cannot pass its required leak or pre-use testing while a patient depends on that ventilator. Transfer the patient to another verified device before testing.

**Expected outcome:** The patient has reliable respiratory support and the suspect circuit can be evaluated independently.

### 2. Confirm the Leak-Test Failure

Repeat the appropriate circuit test under non-patient conditions using the intended patient circuit and required accessories. Record where in the test sequence the failure occurs and any displayed message.

Do not bypass a failed circuit check.

**Expected outcome:** The leak-test failure is confirmed and reproducible. If the circuit passes repeated testing without intervention, inspect for intermittent connections before considering the issue resolved.

### 3. Inspect the Entire Patient Circuit

Trace the breathing circuit from the ventilator to the patient-end connection. Look for disconnected tubing, partially seated fittings, cracked tubing, damaged cuffs or connectors, loose adapters, open sampling ports, or incorrectly installed components.

Reconnect all components securely.

**Expected outcome:** The circuit is complete, correctly assembled, and visibly intact. If reseating a connection allows the test to pass consistently, proceed to final verification and stop troubleshooting afterward.

### 4. Check Circuit Accessories and Open Ports

Inspect all externally attached accessories that could introduce a leak, including adapters, sampling lines, caps, water traps, nebulization interfaces, humidification components, and other approved circuit accessories.

Ensure unused ports that are intended to be closed are appropriately sealed with approved components.

**Expected outcome:** No accessory or open connection creates an unintended leak. If correcting an accessory or open port resolves the test failure, stop after successful repeat testing.

### 5. Inspect Humidification Components

If an external humidification system is part of the circuit, inspect its chamber, tubing interfaces, seals, and connections. Verify the circuit is assembled for the actual configuration being tested.

A loose chamber connection or improperly seated component may mimic a ventilator leak.

**Expected outcome:** Humidification components are correctly installed and leak-free. If the circuit now passes, continue to final verification.

### 6. Verify Flow Sensor and Exhalation Components

Inspect externally accessible flow-sensing and exhalation-valve components for correct orientation, secure seating, contamination, damage, moisture accumulation, or incorrect component selection.

Do not attempt internal sensor or valve repair.

**Expected outcome:** Required circuit components are correctly installed and recognized. If reseating or replacing a defective approved external component resolves the test, proceed to final verification.

### 7. Use a Known-Good Circuit for Isolation

When permitted, substitute a known-good compatible patient circuit and required accessories. Perform the same circuit test without changing unrelated ventilator settings.

This separates a circuit/accessory problem from a ventilator-side problem.

**Expected outcome:** If the known-good circuit passes, the original circuit or one of its accessories is the likely cause. Replace the defective external component and stop troubleshooting after successful verification.

### 8. Check Circuit Configuration and Selected Setup

Verify that the ventilator's externally accessible setup corresponds to the circuit and patient category being evaluated. Do not make unauthorized clinical or service-level configuration changes.

An incorrect setup can cause testing to be performed against the wrong circuit arrangement.

**Expected outcome:** Circuit configuration and physical setup agree. If correcting an authorized setup discrepancy resolves the test failure, complete repeat testing.

### 9. Perform Final Circuit and Ventilator Verification

Run the required circuit check again using the final intended circuit configuration. Confirm the ventilator reaches a ready condition and applicable alarms and ventilation performance checks pass using appropriate test equipment.

**Expected outcome:** The completed circuit passes required checks repeatedly and the ventilator meets applicable performance requirements. Troubleshooting can stop.

### 10. Escalate if a Known-Good Circuit Also Fails

If a verified compatible circuit, properly installed accessories, sensors, and valves still cannot pass the test, do not continue clinical use.

**Expected outcome:** A possible ventilator-side or service-level fault is recognized and the device is removed from service for further evaluation.

## If the Problem Persists

Common circuit assembly, connection, accessory, humidifier, external sensor, valve, and configuration causes have been ruled out. The remaining problem may involve internal pneumatic leakage, pressure or flow measurement, valve control, calibration, or another service-level condition.

The ventilator should be:

- Removed from service.
- Labeled **Out of Service**.
- Sent for repair or bench evaluation.
- Evaluated using approved Vyaire documentation and suitable ventilator test equipment.
- Repaired or configured only by qualified personnel.
- Required to pass applicable circuit, alarm, safety, and ventilation-performance testing before return to service.

Stopping after external circuit causes have been ruled out is proper troubleshooting.

## Clinical Use Tip

Never place a patient on a ventilator using a circuit that cannot successfully complete the required pre-use circuit verification.

## Final Thought

A failed leak test should be approached from the patient circuit inward. Verify every external connection and accessory before assuming a ventilator fault, use known-good substitutions when appropriate, and escalate when the device cannot pass required verification.

That is successful troubleshooting.
