## What This Guide Helps With

Troubleshoots persistent gas-supply alarms caused by hoses, connectors, source selection, restrictions, intermittent supply, or service-level pressure-sensing faults.

## Step-by-Step Troubleshooting

### 1. Protect the Patient and Ensure Reliable Ventilation
Do not troubleshoot a persistent gas-supply alarm while the patient depends on the affected gas path.

Move the patient to a verified ventilator or establish appropriate backup ventilation and gas supply before technical testing.

**Expected outcome:** Patient ventilation continues independently of the affected Servo-n.

### 2. Confirm Which Gas Supply Is Affected
Determine whether the alarm concerns:
- Air
- Oxygen
- Both gases
- Only one wall outlet
- Only one hose or cylinder source

Document whether the alarm is continuous or intermittent.

**Expected outcome:** The affected supply path is clearly identified.

### 3. Verify the Source With Appropriate Test Equipment
Confirm that the gas source actually provides acceptable pressure and flow under the applicable facility and manufacturer requirements.

Do not assume the source is normal solely because another device is connected nearby.

**Expected outcome:** The external gas source is independently confirmed functional.

### 4. Inspect the Supply Hose
Inspect the complete hose for:
- Kinks
- Compression
- Internal restriction signs
- Cracked fittings
- Damaged quick-connects
- Incorrect gas-specific connection
- Loose coupling

Reseat both ends.

**Expected outcome:** The hose is correctly connected and physically intact.

### 5. Try an Alternate Verified Source
When available, connect the ventilator to another known-good wall outlet or approved gas source.

**Expected outcome:** If the alarm clears on the alternate source, investigate the original infrastructure connection rather than the ventilator.

### 6. Try a Known-Good Compatible Hose
Substitute a known-good approved gas hose for the affected supply.

**Expected outcome:** If the alarm clears, remove the original hose from service. Troubleshooting can stop after final verification.

### 7. Inspect Ventilator Gas Inlet Connections
Examine the accessible ventilator inlet for:
- Debris
- Damage
- Loose fittings
- Contamination
- Improperly engaged connectors

Do not probe deeply into the inlet or disassemble internal pneumatic components.

**Expected outcome:** The ventilator inlet is externally clean, undamaged, and properly connected.

### 8. Check for Intermittent Supply Conditions
Observe the system while other high-demand equipment uses the same supply area when appropriate.

If the alarm occurs only intermittently, coordinate with Facilities or Medical Gas personnel to investigate infrastructure pressure or flow instability.

**Expected outcome:** The source remains stable during the test period.

### 9. Perform Final Functional Verification
Run the approved pre-use check and confirm normal operation on both required gas supplies.

Verify the supply-pressure alarm does not recur.

**Expected outcome:** Gas supplies remain recognized and stable and the ventilator passes required testing. Troubleshooting can stop.

## If the Problem Persists

External pressure, source, hose, connector, and infrastructure causes have been ruled out. The remaining problem may involve an internal pressure sensor, inlet assembly, pneumatic restriction, valve system, or associated electronics.

The Servo-n should be:
- Removed from service
- Labeled Out of Service
- Sent for repair or bench evaluation
- Evaluated using appropriate Getinge service documentation and approved pneumatic test equipment
- Repaired or calibrated only by qualified personnel

Return-to-service testing should include gas-supply recognition, alarm verification, pre-use checks, and ventilator performance testing.

Knowing when to stop external troubleshooting and escalate is proper troubleshooting.

## Clinical Use Tip

A normal static wall-pressure reading does not rule out a hose restriction or intermittent supply problem under demand.

## Final Thought

Verify the complete gas path from infrastructure to ventilator before assuming an internal pneumatic fault. Patient safety, independent supply verification, controlled substitution, appropriate escalation, and clear documentation are essential.

That is successful troubleshooting.
