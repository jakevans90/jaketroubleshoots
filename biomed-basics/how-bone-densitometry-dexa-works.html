How Bone Densitometry / DEXA Works

How a bone densitometer uses two X-ray energy ranges to estimate bone mineral content, separate bone from soft tissue, and calculate bone mineral density from a measurement that looks much simpler than it really is

A bone densitometer does not look nearly as intimidating as a CT scanner, angiography system, or MRI.

The Simple Version

A DEXA scanner sends X-rays through the patient and measures how much radiation reaches the detector. The difficulty is that both bone and soft tissue attenuate the beam. With only one X-ray energy, the detector sees the combined attenuation and cannot easily determine how much came from mineralized bone versus surrounding tissue.

DEXA solves this by acquiring information at two different effective X-ray energies. Bone and soft tissue change their attenuation differently as photon energy changes. By comparing the measurements from the two energy ranges, the system mathematically separates much of the soft-tissue contribution and estimates the amount of mineral present in the bone.

The scanner then calculates bone mineral content and divides it by the projected bone area to produce an areal bone mineral density value, commonly expressed in g/cm². That number can then be compared with reference populations to generate T-scores or Z-scores. Because this is a quantitative measurement, calibration, patient positioning, analysis regions, detector stability, and repeatability matter just as much as whether the image looks acceptable. Preserve phantom trends, measured BMD, scan mode, positioning, analysis region, and whether a repeat scan reproduces the result before assuming the X-ray source or detector has failed.

Start With X-Ray Attenuation

As X-rays pass through the patient:

Some photons are absorbed or scattered.

Soft tissue attenuates less.

  • Other tissue

It does not automatically know which attenuation came from bone.

Dual Energy Adds Another Piece of Information

Instead of measuring attenuation at one X-ray energy:

Measure it at two.

Why That Helps

The relationship between attenuation and photon energy differs between:

  • Bone mineral
  • Soft tissue

Bone Contains Higher-Atomic-Number Elements

Especially:

  • Calcium
  • Phosphorus

Energy Dependence

Their attenuation changes differently with X-ray energy than the surrounding soft tissue.

Two Equations Help Solve Two Components

Low-energy measurement

and:

High-energy measurement

  • Bone contribution
  • Soft-tissue contribution

Real-World Example: Phantom Drift

Daily phantom BMD has slowly increased over several weeks.

Technologists have not reported image problems.

The system is repeatable but moving away from baseline.

This is exactly the kind of issue quantitative QC is supposed to detect before clinical confidence is affected.

Real-World Example: Patient Change, Phantom Stable

One patient's spine BMD changes unexpectedly between visits.

Daily QC is stable.

Images show different positioning and degenerative change.

There is little evidence of scanner calibration failure.

Real-World Example: One Scan Mode Fails

Standard scan mode passes phantom QC.

High-resolution mode produces a repeatable streak.

Detector readout configuration or mode-specific acquisition becomes more interesting than the entire scanner.

Real-World Example: BMD Wrong After Service

X-ray tube or detector service completed.

Images look fine.

Phantom BMD is shifted from historical baseline.

Required post-service calibration was not completed.

The system is imaging but not yet quantitatively validated.

Real-World Example: Position-Dependent Artifact

Scan repeatedly develops a streak at the same carriage location.

Artifact occurs with different phantoms.

Motion or cable behavior linked to scanner-arm position becomes suspicious.

Common Mistakes

Thinking DEXA directly measures the physical density of bone in g/cm³. Standard DXA usually reports areal BMD in g/cm².

Judging calibration from image appearance. A quantitative drift may exist with a perfectly recognizable image.

Ignoring patient positioning in serial studies. Geometry can change the measured projected area.

Assuming T-score is a direct detector measurement. The scanner measures BMD; the score comes from comparison with reference data.

Recalibrating every phantom shift without investigating why it occurred. Stable baseline changes deserve explanation.

Comparing two scanners as if their numbers must be identical. Cross-calibration may be necessary.

Treating all unusual patient results as equipment faults. Clinical anatomy, metal, degenerative change, and analysis choices can affect measurements.

Another Useful Troubleshooting Split

Ask:

Is the image wrong, the BMD number wrong, or only the final score/analysis wrong?

Then:

Does the phantom reproduce the change?

Then:

Is the problem stable across scan modes and positions?

Those questions separate:

  • X-ray acquisition
  • Detector
  • Calibration
  • Positioning
  • Analysis software

far better than looking only at the patient report.

What Did You Actually Prove?

If the DEXA image looks normal:

You proved:

The scanner produced a recognizable acquisition under that condition.

You did not prove:

  • BMD calibration accurate
  • Quantitative precision acceptable

If the phantom BMD matches expected baseline:

You have evidence that:

The system's quantitative measurement chain is stable under that controlled phantom condition.

You did not prove:

A patient's positioning or analysis was correct.

If repeated phantom QC, calibration checks, positioning verification, and system-specific performance tests all pass:

You have much stronger evidence that the densitometer is functioning correctly.

Final Thoughts for Biomeds

DEXA looks simple because the scanner is small and the images are not dramatic.

But the measurement problem is sophisticated.

The detector sees:

Bone plus soft tissue.

The system uses two X-ray energies to separate their effects.

It estimates how much mineral is present.

It measures the projected bone area.

Then it calculates BMD.

After that, software compares the result with reference populations and produces scores used clinically.

That means there are several opportunities for a number to become wrong.

The X-ray spectra can drift.

The detector can drift.

Calibration can shift.

The patient can be positioned differently.

The software can analyze a different region.

The reference data can change.

The useful question is not:

Did it make a spine picture?

It is:

Did the entire quantitative measurement chain reproduce the known result it was supposed to reproduce?

That is why the phantom matters.

It gives you something the patient cannot:

A stable known reference.

And that is exactly what you need when the problem is not merely:

Can the scanner see bone?

but:

Can the scanner measure it accurately?

And, as always:

What did you actually prove?

Important Note

DEXA/DXA X-ray generation methods, energy-separation techniques, detector types, beam geometry, calibration phantoms, BMD algorithms, body-composition analysis, reference databases, precision requirements, cross-calibration methods, QC procedures, and acceptable limits vary significantly by manufacturer, scanner model, software version, and clinical program. DEXA uses ionizing radiation. Follow current OEM documentation, approved bone-densitometry QC procedures, facility radiation-safety requirements, qualified medical-physics guidance, and authorized service scope when evaluating quantitative performance.
