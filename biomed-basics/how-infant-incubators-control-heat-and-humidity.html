How Infant Incubators Control Heat and Humidity

How an incubator measures infant and air temperature, moves heated air through a controlled environment, adds humidity, and decides how much heat to apply without allowing a small sensor or airflow problem to become a dangerous thermal condition

An infant incubator can look like a transparent box with a heater underneath it.

The Simple Version

An infant incubator creates a warm controlled environment by heating air and circulating it around the mattress compartment. Internal temperature sensors measure the air, and the controller adjusts heater output to maintain the selected temperature. A circulation fan helps prevent large temperature differences from developing within the enclosure.

Many incubators also provide a skin-temperature or servo mode. In this mode, a temperature probe attached to the infant becomes a major feedback signal. The controller changes heater output according to the measured skin temperature rather than controlling only to a fixed enclosure-air temperature. Because a detached, damaged, or incorrectly placed probe can report a misleading temperature, the system uses alarms and protective limits to reduce the risk of uncontrolled heating.

Humidity systems add water vapor to the circulating air to reduce evaporative losses and maintain the selected environment. A temperature or humidity complaint can therefore originate in the heater, fan, air sensor, skin probe, humidity system, enclosure seal, access door, or controller. Preserve operating mode, selected value, independent air temperature, skin-probe reading, heater output state, fan operation, humidity reading, door status, and alarm history before adjusting calibration or replacing components.

Start With Heat Loss

A newborn loses heat through several physical mechanisms.

Radiation

Heat moves between the infant and cooler surrounding surfaces.

Convection

Moving air carries heat away from the body.

Conduction

Direct contact transfers heat to cooler surfaces.

Evaporation

Moisture evaporating from the skin removes heat.

Premature Infants Can Be Especially Vulnerable

The incubator reduces environmental heat loss by controlling the surrounding conditions.

The Goal Is Not Simply High Air Temperature

It is:

A stable thermal environment.

Enclosure

The clear hood helps isolate the patient from room conditions.

It reduces:

  • Air exchange
  • Drafts
  • Rapid environmental changes

But It Is Not Airtight

Clinicians need access.

Portholes

Allow hands to enter.

Large Doors

Allow patient access.

Every Opening Disturbs the Environment

Warm air escapes.

Cool room air enters.

The Incubator Must Recover

Recovery Time Matters

After a door is opened and closed:

How quickly does the incubator return to the set condition?

Slow Recovery

Could indicate:

  • Weak heater
  • Weak fan
  • Door seal problem

But Some drop is normal

because the enclosure was physically opened.

Air Circulation

Many incubators circulate heated air through the enclosure.

↓

↓

↓

Circulation Fan

The fan distributes heat.

Without Good Circulation

One area could become hot.

Another:

Cool.

Temperature Uniformity

is therefore a major performance characteristic.

A single temperature sensor cannot describe every point in the enclosure

if circulation is poor.

Heater

The heater adds thermal energy.

Usually Resistive

Electrical current produces heat.

Controller Varies Heater Output

according to measured temperature.

Heater Does Not Necessarily Run Fully On or Off Only

It may use:

  • Time-proportional control
  • PWM
  • Multiple power levels

Heater Indicator

Some incubators show:

Heater power percentage.

This Is Valuable Troubleshooting Information

Example

Air temp below setpoint.

Heater output:

100%.

Controller clearly wants more heat.

If temperature still cannot rise

look toward:

  • Heater capacity
  • Airflow
  • Enclosure losses
  • Supply power

Different Example

Air temp low.

Heater output:

0%.

Now control/sensor logic becomes more interesting.

Commanded Behavior Tells You What the Controller Believes

Air Temperature Sensor

The incubator contains one or more air-temperature sensors.

Possible Sensor Types

  • Thermistors
  • RTDs
  • Semiconductor sensors

depending on design.

Sensor Converts Temperature Into Electrical Signal

Controller converts that into:

Displayed air temperature.

Air Sensor Location Matters

It may be located:

  • In circulation path
  • Near mattress area
  • At a defined reference point

That location may not match your random handheld thermometer position.

Test Procedures Specify Measurement Points

for a reason.

Air-Control Mode

In air mode:

The user selects an enclosure-air temperature.

Example

35.0°C.

Sensor Reads

34.4°C.

Controller increases heater output.

As Air Reaches Setpoint

Heater output decreases.

This Is Direct Environmental Control

Infant Temperature Is Not the Primary Feedback

The infant may have a separate monitor.

But air mode is regulating:

Air.

Skin / Servo Mode

In servo mode:

The control target is based on:

Patient skin temperature.

Skin Probe

Attached to infant according to clinical protocol.

Sensor reports:

Patient-surface temperature.

Controller compares with:

Skin-temperature setpoint.

Infant cooler than target

Heater output increases.

Infant warmer

Heater output decreases.

This Is a Very Different Feedback loop.

Air Temperature Can Move More in Servo Mode

because the controller is prioritizing:

Skin measurement.

Important Troubleshooting Point

Do not say:

Air temp isn't holding the setpoint.

if the incubator is actually in skin-control mode.

Ask First:

What mode are we in?

Skin Probe

Usually contains a thermistor or similar sensor.

Probe Integrity Matters

A damaged wire can produce:

  • Open circuit
  • Intermittent reading
  • Offset

But Probe Placement Matters Just as Much

Probe Detached From Infant

Falls into cooler air.

It may read much lower than actual infant skin temperature.

What Does Controller Think?

Patient is cold.

Response

Increase heater output.

Potentially dangerous if not detected.

This Is Why Probe Alarms Matter

Some systems detect implausible conditions

but no sensor can perfectly know where it is physically attached.

Probe Cover

May help maintain skin contact.

If covered incorrectly or exposed to radiant heat

reading may become misleading.

Servo Systems Depend on Sensor Truth

This is the central concept.

A Closed Loop Can Be Dangerous When the Feedback Is Wrong

The controller may be functioning perfectly.

It is simply responding to bad information.

Skin Probe Calibration

A known-temperature simulator or controlled bath may be used depending on OEM procedure.

Compare

Displayed skin temperature

with:

Known reference.

Do Not Warm the probe in your hand and call it calibrated.

Air Probe vs Skin Probe

Separate measurement chains.

One can be accurate while the other is not.

This gives useful isolation.

Air Mode Good, Servo Mode Bad

Skin probe or skin channel becomes more interesting.

Both Modes Bad

Shared heater, airflow, or central control becomes more likely.

Temperature Uniformity

Incubator temperature should be reasonably consistent across specified points.

Why Test Multiple Locations?

A central sensor can read correctly while one corner is:

Too cool.

Possible Cause

Poor circulation.

Uniformity Test

Uses multiple temperature probes positioned according to OEM or standard procedure.

Stabilization Time

Incubators have significant thermal mass.

Mattress

Walls.

Air.

Humidity reservoir.

You must allow enough time for stabilization before judging accuracy.

Measuring Too Early

Produces false failure.

But Extremely Slow Stabilization

Can itself be meaningful.

Airflow

Fan performance strongly affects:

  • Uniformity
  • Recovery
  • Heater temperature

Fan Failure

Can create:

Local overheating near heater.

Safety logic should detect unsafe conditions.

Fan Tachometer

May provide feedback.

Fan spins

but tach signal missing.

Controller may shut heater down.

User complaint:

Incubator stays cold.

Root cause:

Fan feedback.

Dirty Air Path

Lint or debris can reduce circulation.

Filter

If present, may become clogged.

Reduced airflow

can cause:

  • Poor uniformity
  • Slow recovery
  • Heater overtemperature

Cleanliness Matters

Especially in neonatal equipment.

Heater Overtemperature Sensor

Independent sensor may monitor heater or air path.

If airflow is low

heater-area temperature rises.

Safety system shuts heater down.

Again

Cold incubator can originate from:

A real overtemperature condition internally.

High Air Temperature Alarm

If enclosure gets too hot:

Alarm.

Heater should be limited or disabled.

Low Air Temperature Alarm

If temperature falls outside allowed range:

Alarm.

Alarm delays may exist

to prevent nuisance alarm immediately after door opening.

Alarm Thresholds Depend on Mode

and manufacturer.

Do Not assume one universal number.

Independent Overtemperature Cutout

Many incubators use a second safety circuit separate from the main control loop.

Why?

Main controller or sensor could fail.

Independent protection

reduces risk of runaway heating.

Thermal Fuse / Thermostat

May provide another layer.

Multiple Safety Layers

are common because the patient population is vulnerable.

Does Passing Main Temperature Calibration Prove Overtemperature Protection?

No.

Separate tests are required.

Humidity

Warm air can be dry.

Dry Environment

Can increase evaporative water loss.

Humidification adds water vapor.

Relative Humidity

Describes how much water vapor is present relative to the maximum the air could hold at that temperature.

Warm Air Can Hold More Moisture

This means humidity percentage depends on temperature.

70% RH at one temperature

does not represent the same absolute water content as 70% at another.

Humidity Control

The incubator may use:

  • Heated water reservoir
  • Evaporative surface
  • Controlled airflow

Exact method varies.

Water Reservoir

Needs:

  • Correct fill
  • Correct installation
  • Proper cleaning

Empty Reservoir

Humidity target cannot be reached.

Heater Can Still maintain temperature normally.

This is a separate subsystem.

Humidity Heater

Some systems heat water to increase evaporation.

Heater failure

produces:

Low humidity.

Fan / Airflow Failure

Can also reduce moisture distribution.

Humidity Sensor

Measures relative humidity in the enclosure or air path.

Sensor Drift

Can create:

Stable but inaccurate RH reading.

Controller may then add too much or too little humidity.

Independent Humidity Meter

Can verify actual conditions.

Again

Displayed value is only a measurement.

Humidity Recovery

Opening a door allows moist air to escape rapidly.

Humidity may recover more slowly than temperature.

That can be normal.

Condensation

If surfaces are cooler than the dew point:

Water condenses.

Excessive Condensation

May indicate:

  • High humidity
  • Cold surface
  • Airflow issue

Condensation near electronics is especially undesirable.

Humidity Does Not Mean Wet Everything

The system controls vapor concentration.

Reservoir Contamination

Warm water systems can create infection-control concerns.

Cleaning procedures are critical.

Mineral Deposits

Can affect:

  • Heater
  • Water level sensor
  • Reservoir

depending on water requirements.

Use specified water type.

Do not assume tap water is acceptable.

Water Level Sensor

Some systems detect low water.

Float

Conductivity sensor

or other method may be used.

Low-Water Alarm

Can disable humidification.

Temperature control may remain functional.

User says:

Humidity broken.

Check reservoir and level detection before control boards.

Door Sensors

Some incubators may know when:

Door / access panel is open.

Why?

Can:

  • Alarm
  • Modify control
  • Suppress certain alarms temporarily

Door Switch Failure

System may think door is open continuously.

That can alter environmental-control behavior.

Door Seal

Mechanical seal can leak air.

Heater runs harder.

Temperature may still reach setpoint

but recovery and power use change.

Historical Heater Percentage

Can reveal this.

Same room.

Same setpoint.

Used to require:

30% heater.

Now:

70%.

Something changed.

Possible Causes

  • Room colder
  • Enclosure leak
  • Heater degradation
  • Fan issue

Servo Mode Can Hide Environmental Changes

If skin temperature remains controlled

the incubator may compensate by changing air temperature.

Again

Feedback systems hide degradation.

Mattress

Mattress and accessories affect airflow around infant.

Wrong accessory

can block vents.

Added Equipment

Blankets or devices can obstruct air paths.

Clinical setup can influence environmental performance.

Radiation Warmer Combination

Some hybrid devices function as both:

  • Incubator
  • Radiant warmer

In closed mode

incubator airflow controls temperature.

In open mode

radiant heater may provide heat from above.

Completely different thermal path.

Mode matters enormously.

Do Not Troubleshoot an Open-Warmer Mode Like a Closed Incubator

Hood Lift

Hybrid system may raise hood.

Position sensors confirm configuration.

Heater-control logic changes.

Hood Sensor Fault

can prevent mode transition.

Oxygen Control

Some incubators include oxygen-control capability.

This is a separate but related environmental system.

Oxygen Source

may enter enclosure.

Oxygen Sensor

measures concentration.

Controller adjusts valve.

Similar closed-loop concept

but with gas concentration instead of temperature.

Oxygen-enriched environments introduce fire risk.

Follow OEM and facility procedures.

Do not improvise oxygen testing.

Alarm Audio

Temperature control can be perfect.

Alarm speaker can still fail.

Audible alarm is safety-critical.

PM should verify alarm functions required by manufacturer.

Power Failure Alarm

Incubator may include battery-backed alarm if AC power is lost.

Main heater usually cannot run long from a small backup battery

depending on design.

But alarm must still alert staff.

Battery

Some transport incubators use substantial battery power.

Stationary incubator and transport incubator architectures differ.

Transport Incubator

Must maintain heat while moving between areas.

Batteries

Become much more important.

Weak Battery

Can produce:

  • Short runtime
  • Heater limitation

Fan and display may work

while heater drains battery quickly.

Battery Testing Should Reflect Load

External Power

Some transport incubators support:

  • AC
  • DC vehicle power
  • Battery

Source switching faults

can create intermittent temperature control during transport.

Temperature Calibration

Use a controlled and traceable test method.

Calibration may involve:

  • Air sensor
  • Skin channel
  • Overtemperature limit

Each should be considered separately.

Do Not Adjust the Main Sensor to Fix Poor Uniformity

If center reads correct

but corners are wrong:

That is not simply calibration.

Investigate airflow.

Uniformity vs Accuracy

Different properties.

Accuracy

Measured temperature close to reference.

Uniformity

Different locations close to one another.

Stability

Temperature does not fluctuate excessively over time.

A good incubator needs all three.

Recovery

How quickly it returns after disturbance.

Four different performance concepts.

One thermometer at one moment proves very little.

Sensor Response Time

A reference thermometer with slow response can make incubator regulation look unstable.

Test instrumentation matters.

Probe Placement

Reference sensors must not:

Touch walls.

Sit directly in heater air outlet

unless procedure says so.

Radiant influence

can affect readings.

Real-World Example: Air Mode Good, Servo Mode Runs Hot

Air-control testing passes.

In servo mode, heater drives unusually hard.

Skin simulator shows the skin channel reads 1.5°C low.

Controller is doing exactly what bad feedback tells it to do.

Real-World Example: Heater at 100%, Temperature Won't Recover

Doors closed.

Setpoint correct.

Heater command stays at maximum.

Air temperature rises very slowly.

Circulation fan speed is far below normal.

Heat is not being distributed through the enclosure efficiently.

Real-World Example: Center Temperature Passes, Corner Fails

Main display agrees with center reference.

One side of mattress compartment remains significantly cooler.

Airflow path is partially blocked.

Recalibrating the air sensor would make the center wrong and would not repair the actual problem.

Real-World Example: Humidity Never Reaches Setpoint

Temperature control is normal.

Humidity remains low.

Reservoir is full.

Independent RH meter confirms the display.

Humidity heater has no current draw.

The failure is localized to humidification rather than the entire incubator.

Real-World Example: Overtemperature Alarm After Cleaning

Incubator runs for several minutes and shuts down with high-temperature alarm.

Circulation fan intake filter was reinstalled incorrectly after cleaning and severely restricts airflow.

Safety system is responding to a real thermal condition.

Common Mistakes

Troubleshooting without identifying air mode vs skin mode. They use different feedback targets.

Assuming the displayed air temperature represents every location in the enclosure. Uniformity must be verified separately.

Replacing a heater because the incubator stays cold. Fan or airflow faults can disable heat or prevent distribution.

Treating a detached skin probe as a simple low-temperature reading. In servo mode it can drive the control system toward more heat.

Calibrating the main sensor to fix poor uniformity. Calibration cannot repair airflow.

Judging humidity immediately after opening the enclosure. Humidity recovery can take time.

Treating passing startup self-test as complete thermal validation. Independent temperature, uniformity, alarm, and humidity tests provide stronger evidence.

Another Useful Troubleshooting Split

Ask:

Which control mode is active?

Then:

Is the measured problem air temperature, skin temperature, humidity, or all three?

Then:

Is the controller commanding more heat or less heat?

Then:

Do independent measurements agree with the incubator's sensors?

Those questions separate:

  • Heater
  • Airflow
  • Sensor
  • Humidity
  • Control logic

far faster than starting with parts.

What Did You Actually Prove?

If the incubator display says:

36.0°C,

you proved:

The incubator's measurement system reports 36.0°C at its sensing point.

You did not prove:

  • Entire mattress area is 36.0°C
  • Skin probe accurate
  • Overtemperature protection functional

If an independent calibrated probe agrees at the specified test location:

You have stronger evidence of air-temperature accuracy there.

If multiple reference probes demonstrate correct accuracy, uniformity, stability, recovery, alarms, and humidity performance according to the required test:

You have much stronger evidence that the complete incubator environmental-control system is functioning correctly.

Final Thoughts for Biomeds

An incubator is not simply trying to make a box warm.

It is managing an environment.

The heater creates thermal energy.

The fan distributes it.

The air sensor tells the controller what the enclosure is doing.

The skin probe can tell the controller what the patient is doing.

The humidity system changes evaporative conditions.

The enclosure limits exchange with the room.

The safety system watches for the moment those pieces stop agreeing.

That is why the first question in an incubator complaint should often be:

Which temperature are we talking about?

Air temperature?

Skin temperature?

Setpoint?

Independent reference?

Those are not interchangeable.

And neither are:

Accuracy,

uniformity,

stability,

and recovery.

A machine can pass one and fail another.

The most dangerous mistake is assuming that because the controller is responding logically, its input must be correct.

A servo loop can respond perfectly to a bad sensor.

That principle applies everywhere in medical equipment, but an incubator makes it especially obvious.

The controller only knows what its sensors tell it.

So when the display confidently says:

36.5°C,

the biomed question remains:

What did you actually prove?

Important Note

Infant-incubator heater designs, circulation systems, air and skin temperature sensors, servo-control algorithms, humidity systems, oxygen-control options, overtemperature protections, alarm thresholds, transport-power systems, test locations, stabilization times, and acceptable performance limits vary significantly by manufacturer and model. Incubators directly control the thermal environment of highly vulnerable patients. Follow current OEM documentation, approved temperature/humidity testing procedures, neonatal equipment safety requirements, infection-control procedures, and authorized service scope when evaluating incubator performance.
