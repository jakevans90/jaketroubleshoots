Calibration Stickers: What They Do and Don’t Mean

Why a calibration sticker can show that a device was serviced without proving the device is accurate right now

Calibration stickers are common on medical equipment.

The Simple Version

Think of the process like this:

Device is tested

↓

Results are compared with specification

↓

Device passes, fails, or is adjusted

↓

Work is documented

↓

Sticker may be applied

The sticker is:

Evidence of the documented service event.

It is not the measurement itself.

What Is Calibration?

Calibration generally involves comparing the output of an instrument or device against a known reference.

Depending on the equipment and procedure, calibration may involve:

  • Measuring accuracy
  • Comparing against a standard
  • Adjusting the device
  • Recording results

Calibration Does Not Always Mean Adjustment

This is an important distinction.

A device may be tested and found:

Within specification.

No adjustment is needed.

That can still be part of a calibration process.

Verification

Verification asks:

Does the device meet the required specification?

Example:

Defibrillator set to:

200 J.

Analyzer measures:

198 J.

Manufacturer tolerance:

Within acceptable range.

The output passes.

Adjustment

Adjustment means changing the device to bring its performance closer to the desired value.

Example:

Temperature channel reads:

2°C high.

An authorized calibration adjustment is performed.

After adjustment:

Reading falls within specification.

Calibration vs PM

Preventive maintenance and calibration are related but not identical.

A PM may include:

  • Visual inspection
  • Cleaning
  • Functional testing
  • Battery checks
  • Calibration verification

Some equipment needs very little true calibration.

Other equipment requires significant measurement verification.

One Sticker May Represent Several Things

A label might mean:

  • PM completed
  • Calibration completed
  • Safety inspection completed
  • Combination of these

Know your facility's labeling system.

Do Not Assume “PM Sticker” Means “Calibrated”

If the sticker only indicates preventive maintenance:

It may not represent a calibration event at all.

What Is a Calibration Sticker?

A calibration sticker typically documents that a calibration-related service event occurred.

It may include:

  • Calibration date
  • Due date
  • Technician
  • Department
  • Status

What Does the Date Mean?

Usually:

The date reflects when the service event was completed.

It does not mean the device will remain accurate until the next due date.

Example

Calibration completed:

January 1.

Device dropped:

January 10.

Sticker still says:

Due next January.

The sticker did not protect the device from damage.

Sticker Does Not Freeze Device Condition

Equipment can drift or fail after service.

Possible causes include:

  • Mechanical impact
  • Component aging
  • Sensor drift
  • Repair
  • Environmental exposure

Due Date

A due date generally reflects the maintenance or calibration interval assigned to the device.

It is not an expiration date in the same way as medication expiration.

Due Date Means

Conceptually:

This device is scheduled to be serviced again by this date according to the applicable maintenance program.

It Does Not Mean

Device automatically becomes inaccurate at midnight on the due date.

Overdue Status

An overdue sticker is primarily a:

Maintenance control issue.

You need to determine what your facility policy requires.

Do Not Assume Overdue Means Broken

A device can be overdue and still function perfectly.

But the required scheduled maintenance has not been completed within the assigned interval.

Do Not Assume Current Sticker Means Good Device

The reverse is also true.

A device can have a current sticker and still be defective today.

Example

Infusion pump PM:

Passed last month.

Today:

Motor mechanism slips.

Sticker still current.

Current sticker does not override today's failure.

Test Results Matter More Than Label Appearance

If a clinician reports incorrect performance:

Investigate the complaint.

Do not say:

It was calibrated six months ago, so it can't be wrong.

Sticker Is Administrative Evidence

A label is useful for quickly identifying:

  • Service status
  • Next scheduled action

The actual technical evidence lives in:

  • Work order
  • Test record
  • Calibration data
  • Analyzer results

The Work Order Matters

A strong maintenance record may tell you:

  • Test equipment used
  • Actual measured values
  • Pass/fail limits
  • Adjustments performed
  • Parts replaced

The sticker cannot contain all of that information.

Pass/Fail

Suppose the sticker says:

Calibrated.

You still need to know:

What tests were performed?

Example

Patient monitor has:

  • ECG
  • NIBP
  • SpO2
  • Temperature

A calibration event may have tested some channels differently than others.

Test Scope Matters

Never assume:

Calibrated device

means:

Every function independently checked for absolute accuracy.

Review the actual procedure.

Traceability

Calibration often depends on traceability of the reference equipment used.

Simple Traceability Chain

Think:

Medical Device

↓

Test Analyzer

↓

Calibration Laboratory

↓

Recognized Reference Standard

Why This Matters

If your analyzer is wrong:

You can falsely pass or fail the medical device.

Example

Infusion analyzer reads:

5% low.

You test ten pumps.

They all appear:

5% high.

The pumps may be fine.

The reference may be the problem.

Calibration Sticker on Test Equipment

Your analyzer may also have its own:

  • Calibration date
  • Due date
  • Certificate

Calibrated Analyzer Does Not Mean Perfect Analyzer

It means the analyzer was evaluated against applicable requirements.

Every measurement still has uncertainty.

Measurement Uncertainty

No real measurement is infinitely precise.

Example:

Reference says:

100.0 ± 0.5 units.

That uncertainty matters near pass/fail boundaries.

Stickers Do Not Show Measurement Uncertainty

The underlying calibration documentation may.

Out of Tolerance

Suppose a device is tested and found outside specification.

That is:

Out of tolerance.

What Happens Next?

Depending on procedure:

  • Adjust
  • Repair
  • Remove from service
  • Replace component

Then retest.

Sticker Should Reflect Final Status

You should not apply a normal passing calibration label to equipment that still fails required specifications.

As-Found vs As-Left

Calibration documentation may distinguish:

As-found

and:

As-left.

As-Found

How the device performed:

Before adjustment or repair.

As-Left

How the device performed:

After service was completed.

Why As-Found Matters

Suppose device is found:

20% out of tolerance.

You adjust it and it passes.

The final sticker says:

Calibrated.

But the as-found result may have important significance.

Historical Performance

Repeated out-of-tolerance findings can reveal:

  • Sensor drift
  • Mechanical wear
  • Unstable component

The sticker alone hides that history.

Calibration Interval

Intervals may be determined by:

  • Manufacturer recommendation
  • AEM program
  • Risk
  • Service history
  • Regulation or policy

Not Every Device Has the Same Interval

Examples may include:

  • 6 months
  • 12 months
  • 24 months

depending on program and equipment.

Sticker Format Is Facility-Specific

One hospital may use:

PM Due 09/2027.

Another:

Inspected 09/2026.

Another may use:

  • Barcode only
  • Electronic tracking

No Sticker Does Not Automatically Mean No Maintenance

Some organizations rely heavily on electronic records.

Check facility policy.

Sticker Missing

Possible explanations:

  • Fell off
  • Removed during cleaning
  • Equipment newly arrived
  • Service not completed
  • Electronic-labeling system

Do not guess.

Damaged Sticker

A damaged or unreadable label may create:

  • Identification problem
  • Due-date ambiguity

Check the CMMS.

CMMS

The computerized maintenance management system should generally be the stronger source of service history.

Sticker vs CMMS Conflict

Example:

Sticker:

Due December.

CMMS:

Overdue since June.

Do not automatically trust the sticker.

Investigate the record discrepancy.

Wrong Sticker on Device

Labels can accidentally be placed on the wrong asset.

Confirm:

  • Asset number
  • Serial number

before relying on the label.

Calibration After Repair

Some repairs can affect calibration.

Examples:

  • Sensor replacement
  • Main board replacement
  • Pressure transducer replacement

A device may require calibration verification after service even if the normal PM due date is months away.

Current Sticker Does Not Eliminate Post-Repair Testing

The repair changed the system.

Verify what the manufacturer requires.

Example

NIBP board replaced.

PM sticker:

Current.

You still need to verify NIBP performance.

Calibration After Impact

Physical damage may also justify re-verification.

Example:

Precision scale dropped.

Sticker:

Current.

Accuracy now needs evaluation.

Calibration Sticker Is Not Safety Clearance

A sticker does not automatically prove:

  • Electrical safety
  • Physical integrity
  • Alarm function

unless those items were explicitly part of the service event.

“Calibrated” Does Not Mean “Safe”

A device may measure accurately but have:

  • Cracked enclosure
  • Damaged power cord
  • Failed alarm speaker

Accuracy is only one part of equipment condition.

“Passed PM” Does Not Mean “Will Not Fail Tomorrow”

PM reduces risk and verifies current condition.

It does not guarantee future reliability.

Calibration Certificate

Some equipment or test instruments may have a detailed calibration certificate.

This can include:

  • Standard used
  • Measurement results
  • Uncertainty
  • Environmental conditions
  • Laboratory information

That documentation provides far more technical information than the sticker.

Calibration Certificate vs Sticker

Think:

Sticker = quick status indicator.

Certificate/record = technical evidence.

Test Equipment Serial Number

Good calibration records may identify the exact analyzer used.

This supports traceability.

Example

Defibrillator tested with:

Analyzer Serial 12345.

Later, analyzer found out of tolerance.

Now you can identify which devices were tested with it.

This Is Why Documentation Matters

The sticker alone would not tell you that.

Calibration Label Removal

If equipment fails and is removed from service, facility procedure may require:

  • Failure label
  • Quarantine label

rather than leaving a normal service sticker as the only visible status.

Do Not Let a Green Sticker Override an Out-of-Service Tag

Current failure status takes priority.

Clinical Staff Interpretation

Clinicians may sometimes assume:

Sticker means biomed guarantees it works.

Explain when necessary:

The sticker represents scheduled service status, not immunity from failure.

Audit Perspective

Maintenance labels can provide quick evidence during an inspection that equipment is being managed.

But the underlying:

  • Records
  • Procedures
  • Test documentation

matter.

Avoid Sticker-Driven Maintenance

Do not perform maintenance simply to:

Put a new sticker on it.

The goal is verification of equipment performance and condition.

PM Is a Technical Process

The sticker comes at the end.

Common Mistakes

Assuming a Current Sticker Means the Device Cannot Be Faulty

Investigate current symptoms.

Treating Calibration and PM as Identical

Know what the procedure actually includes.

Ignoring As-Found Results

They can reveal drift.

Applying Labels Without Complete Documentation

Sticker and record should agree.

Trusting Sticker Over CMMS Without Investigating

Resolve discrepancies.

Assuming Due Date Is a Guarantee of Accuracy

It is a maintenance interval.

Calibrating With Out-of-Date Test Equipment

Your reference matters.

A Useful Calibration-Label Framework

When you look at a sticker, ask:

What service event does this label represent?

Then:

When was it performed?

Then:

What procedure was used?

Then:

What actual results were recorded?

Then:

What reference equipment was used?

That takes you from:

Sticker

to:

Evidence.

Another Useful Question

Ask:

If I removed the sticker, what technical evidence would still prove this device met specification?

The answer should be:

The service record and test results.

Not:

Nothing.

What Did You Actually Prove?

If a device has a current calibration sticker:

You proved:

Someone labeled it as having completed the applicable calibration/service process on the documented date.

You did not independently prove:

The device is accurate right now.

If you review the maintenance record and see actual measurements within specification:

You proved much more about its condition at the time of that test.

If you independently test it today and it passes:

Now you have evidence of its current performance.

Final Thoughts for Biomeds

Calibration stickers are useful.

They provide a fast visual indication of maintenance status.

But the sticker is not the calibration.

Think:

Measurement → Comparison → Adjustment if needed → Verification → Documentation → Sticker.

The technical work happens before the label goes on.

So when someone points to a current sticker and says:

It was calibrated, so it has to be good,

remember:

Equipment can change.

Sensors can drift.

Components can fail.

And a label is only as meaningful as the process and documentation behind it.

As always:

What did you actually prove?

— Jake

Important Note

Calibration terminology, labeling practices, maintenance intervals, documentation requirements, traceability systems, and return-to-service procedures vary by manufacturer, healthcare organization, test-equipment program, and applicable regulatory requirements. Follow facility policy and current manufacturer procedures when calibrating or labeling medical equipment.
