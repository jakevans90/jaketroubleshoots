What Degree Do You Need to Become a Biomed?

A practical look at biomedical equipment technology degrees, electronics programs, engineering degrees, military training, and whether college is actually required to work in HTM

One of the first questions people ask about becoming a biomedical equipment technician is:

The Simple Version

For many people, one of the most direct educational paths is:

Associate degree in Biomedical Equipment Technology or a closely related electronics field

↓

Entry-level BMET position

↓

Hands-on medical equipment experience

↓

Specialized training and certifications later

But that is not the only route.

You may also enter biomed through:

  • Electronics
  • Military technical training
  • Field service
  • Other technical healthcare experience

The exact requirement depends on the employer.

Do You Need a Degree to Become a Biomed?

Not always.

There is no universal rule saying:

Every biomedical equipment technician must have this exact degree.

Employers create their own requirements.

A job posting may say:

  • Associate degree required
  • Associate degree preferred
  • Technical degree or equivalent experience
  • Military training accepted
  • Related experience accepted in place of education

Read the actual position requirements.

Why Do So Many Biomed Jobs Mention an Associate Degree?

Because the job requires a mix of:

  • Electronics
  • Troubleshooting
  • Medical-device knowledge
  • Safety
  • Networking
  • Documentation

A good two-year technical program can provide a strong foundation in those areas.

Biomedical Equipment Technology

A degree specifically in:

  • Biomedical Equipment Technology

is one of the most directly related options.

Programs may include coursework in:

  • Electronics
  • Digital circuits
  • Medical instrumentation
  • Anatomy and physiology
  • Medical-device safety
  • Troubleshooting
  • Networking
  • Test equipment
  • Why This Path Makes Sense

The coursework is designed around the actual job.

Instead of learning electronics in isolation, you may apply it to equipment such as:

  • ECG systems
  • Patient monitors
  • Defibrillators
  • Infusion pumps

That can make the transition into an HTM shop easier.

Biomedical Engineering Technology

Another closely related program is:

Biomedical Engineering Technology.

Despite the similar name, this may be much more technician-oriented than a traditional biomedical engineering degree.

Programs can emphasize:

  • Electronics
  • Instrumentation
  • Equipment maintenance
  • Troubleshooting

Always look at the curriculum instead of judging the program only by its title.

Program Names Are Not Standardized

One college may call its program:

Biomedical Equipment Technology.

Another may call something very similar:

Biomedical Engineering Technology.

Look at what you will actually study.

Electronics Technology

An Electronics Technology degree can also be a strong route into biomed.

Medical equipment is full of:

  • Power supplies
  • Sensors
  • Relays
  • Motors
  • Digital circuits
  • Communication interfaces

Strong electronics fundamentals transfer well.

What You May Need to Add

An electronics program may not teach much about:

  • Medical terminology
  • Anatomy
  • Clinical workflow
  • Medical-device regulations

Those areas can be learned later through:

  • Employment
  • Additional coursework
  • Manufacturer training
  • Electrical Engineering Technology

Electrical Engineering Technology can also provide useful preparation.

It commonly emphasizes practical application of:

  • Circuits
  • Electronics
  • Measurement
  • Controls

It can provide a solid technical base for troubleshooting equipment.

Electrical Engineering

A traditional electrical engineering degree is more advanced than most entry-level BMET positions require.

It can certainly qualify someone technically.

But if your sole goal is:

  • Become a hospital biomed technician

you usually do not need a four-year electrical engineering degree just to enter the field.

Biomedical Engineering

This is one of the most common points of confusion.

Biomedical Engineering and Biomedical Equipment Technology are not the same thing.

Biomedical Engineering Degree

Biomedical engineering programs often focus more heavily on:

  • Engineering mathematics
  • Design
  • Research
  • Physiology
  • Medical-device development

Graduates may work in:

  • Medical-device companies
  • Research
  • Product development
  • Engineering
  • Biomedical Equipment Technician

A BMET is typically focused on:

  • Maintaining equipment
  • Troubleshooting
  • Testing
  • Repair
  • Installation
  • Clinical support

That is a different role.

Can Someone With a Biomedical Engineering Degree Become a Biomed?

Yes.

But the degree may not teach as much hands-on equipment servicing as a technician-focused program.

Someone with a BME degree may still need to learn:

  • Test equipment
  • Service procedures
  • PM work
  • Equipment-specific troubleshooting
  • Associate Degree vs Bachelor's Degree

For many entry-level BMET roles, an associate degree may provide enough formal education.

A bachelor's degree can be useful for:

  • Advancement
  • Management
  • Engineering roles
  • Broader career options

But it is not automatically necessary to become a good technician.

More School Does Not Automatically Mean Better Troubleshooter

Someone with four years of engineering education can still struggle with:

  • Why won't this monitor power on?

Hands-on troubleshooting is its own skill.

Certificate Programs

Some schools offer:

  • Biomedical equipment certificates
  • Electronics certificates
  • Medical equipment service programs

These may be shorter than a full associate degree.

Are Certificates Enough?

Sometimes.

It depends heavily on:

  • Program quality
  • Employer requirements
  • Your previous experience

A certificate paired with strong electronics experience may be very useful.

A certificate with very little hands-on training may carry less weight.

Look at the Coursework

Before paying for a program, check whether it actually covers:

  • Basic electronics
  • Digital electronics
  • Troubleshooting
  • Medical instrumentation
  • Networking
  • Test equipment
  • Hands-on labs

A title containing:

  • Biomedical

does not automatically make a program good.

Hands-On Labs Matter

Biomed is a hands-on profession.

A strong program should ideally give you experience with:

  • Multimeters
  • Oscilloscopes
  • Power supplies
  • Test equipment
  • Real or simulated medical devices

Reading alone is not the same as troubleshooting equipment.

Anatomy and Physiology

A biomed program may include basic anatomy and physiology.

This helps you understand what the device is measuring.

For example:

ECG equipment makes more sense if you understand basic cardiac electrical activity.

Do You Need Advanced Medical Education?

No.

A BMET does not need the same medical education as:

  • Nurse
  • Physician
  • Respiratory therapist

You need enough to understand:

  • Device purpose
  • Parameters
  • Clinical context
  • Networking Coursework

Networking is increasingly valuable.

Look for programs that teach:

  • Ethernet
  • IP addressing
  • Subnets
  • DHCP
  • Basic network troubleshooting

Modern medical equipment is increasingly connected.

Computer Skills

Useful coursework may also include:

  • Operating systems
  • Computer hardware
  • Basic cybersecurity
  • Software troubleshooting

Modern biomed work extends beyond traditional electronics.

Military Training

Military biomedical-equipment training has been an important path into civilian HTM.

Someone may leave military service with experience in:

  • Medical equipment maintenance
  • Electronics
  • Preventive maintenance
  • Troubleshooting

Employers may recognize this as equivalent to formal civilian education depending on the role.

Related Technical Experience

Another person may enter the field after working in:

  • Electronics repair
  • Field service
  • Industrial maintenance
  • Medical-device manufacturing

Experience can sometimes substitute for formal education.

Why Experience Matters

If you already know how to:

  • Use a multimeter
  • Read schematics
  • Troubleshoot circuits
  • Work safely around electronics

you already possess skills relevant to biomed.

The medical-device side can be learned.

Can You Become a Biomed With No Degree and No Technical Experience?

It is possible, but it is a harder path.

You need some way to demonstrate:

  • Technical aptitude
  • Troubleshooting ability
  • Electronics knowledge

A formal program can help establish that foundation.

Entry-Level Job Requirements

Look at real job descriptions in the area where you want to work.

Notice patterns.

Do employers repeatedly ask for:

  • Associate degree?
  • Two years' experience?
  • Military equivalent?
  • Electronics background?

That tells you more than generic career advice.

Do Not Choose a Degree Based Only on the Word “Biomedical”

Compare actual curricula.

Program A:

  • Biomedical Engineering

may be mostly:

  • Calculus
  • Physics
  • Design

Program B:

  • Electronics Technology

may include:

  • Circuits
  • Troubleshooting
  • Instrumentation
  • Networking

For BMET work, Program B could actually be the more directly useful path.

What Should a Good Biomed Program Teach?

A useful foundation would include subjects such as:

  • DC circuits
  • AC circuits
  • Semiconductor basics
  • Digital electronics
  • Sensors
  • Power supplies
  • Test equipment
  • Computer hardware
  • Networking
  • Anatomy and physiology
  • Medical instrumentation
  • Safety
  • Troubleshooting Should Be Part of the Program

The technician needs to learn more than:

This is what a resistor does.

They should also learn:

How do I determine whether the resistor is actually related to this failure?

The reasoning is what turns electronics knowledge into repair ability.

Accreditation

Depending on the program and your goals, accreditation may matter.

Before enrolling, research:

  • School accreditation
  • Program reputation
  • Transferability of credits
  • Employer recognition

Especially if you may continue toward a bachelor's degree later.

Community Colleges Can Be a Good Option

Many technical associate programs are offered through community colleges.

Advantages may include:

  • Lower tuition
  • Hands-on labs
  • Local employer relationships
  • Internships

You do not necessarily need an expensive university education to enter the field.

Internship Opportunities

A program connected with:

  • Hospitals
  • Medical-device companies
  • Service organizations

may offer valuable internship experience.

That can help bridge:

  • School

and:

First BMET job.

Internship Experience Can Be Huge

A student who graduates already knowing how a clinical engineering shop works may have an advantage.

You may learn:

  • CMMS documentation
  • PM workflows
  • Clinical communication
  • Equipment handling

before your first full-time job.

Degree vs Certification

A degree and a professional certification are not the same thing.

A degree represents:

Formal education.

A professional certification generally represents:

Meeting eligibility requirements and demonstrating knowledge against a defined certification standard.

They serve different purposes.

Do Not Pick a Degree Just to Qualify for a Certification

Think about your actual career foundation first.

Build the knowledge you need to do the work.

Certifications can come later.

OEM Training Is Not a Replacement for Fundamentals

You may eventually attend manufacturer training for:

  • Ventilators
  • Anesthesia machines
  • Patient monitors

But OEM school assumes you already have certain foundational knowledge.

If you understand electronics and troubleshooting, equipment-specific training becomes much easier.

A Strong Foundation Transfers Between Manufacturers

Suppose you understand:

  • Pressure sensors
  • Flow sensors
  • Power supplies
  • Network communication

Those concepts transfer across many models.

Manufacturer menus change.

Fundamentals do not.

What About Online Degrees?

Online programs can provide valuable theory.

But consider how the program handles hands-on technical training.

Biomed requires physical skills such as:

  • Test equipment use
  • Cable inspection
  • Measurement
  • Equipment disassembly

A program needs some way to develop practical competence.

Avoid Degree Mills

Be cautious of programs promising:

Become a biomedical technician in a few weeks with no prior knowledge.

The field requires real technical competence.

There is a difference between:

  • Learning terminology

and:

Being able to safely troubleshoot medical equipment.

Do You Need a Bachelor's Degree to Advance?

Not always.

Many experienced technicians advance through:

  • Experience
  • Certification
  • OEM training
  • Leadership

But some organizations may require bachelor's degrees for certain:

  • Management
  • Engineering
  • Administrative

roles.

Think Long-Term

If you may eventually want to become:

  • Clinical engineering manager
  • Director
  • Engineer

a bachelor's degree may provide more options.

That does not mean you need one before starting as a BMET.

You Can Start With an Associate Degree

A practical path can be:

  • Associate degree
  • ↓
  • Get into the field
  • ↓
  • Build experience
  • ↓
  • Continue education later if needed

That allows you to start earning real HTM experience sooner.

Employer Tuition Assistance

Some employers offer education benefits.

A technician may enter with a two-year degree and later complete additional schooling while working.

If available, that can be a practical strategy.

What If You Already Have a Bachelor's Degree in Something Else?

You may not necessarily need another four-year degree.

If your existing degree is unrelated, consider what technical knowledge you are missing.

You might need:

  • Electronics coursework
  • Certificate
  • Hands-on training

rather than starting over completely.

What If You Already Work in IT?

IT experience can be extremely useful for:

  • Networked patient monitors
  • Device integration
  • Servers
  • Cybersecurity

But you may need to build knowledge in:

  • Electronics
  • Medical instrumentation
  • Test equipment
  • Electrical safety
  • What If You Are an Electrician?

You may already understand:

  • AC power
  • Wiring
  • Electrical safety

But medical electronics involves more:

  • Low-voltage circuits
  • Sensors
  • Digital systems
  • Measurement

Your existing experience is useful, but not complete preparation.

What If You Work on Cars or Industrial Equipment?

Mechanical and troubleshooting skills can transfer surprisingly well.

You may already think in terms of:

  • Inputs
  • Outputs
  • Sensors
  • Control systems

You will need to learn the medical and electronics side.

The Best Degree Is the One That Gets You the Right Fundamentals

Do not obsess over finding the perfect title.

Look for education that gives you:

Electronics + Troubleshooting + Hands-On Skills + Medical Context.

That combination is much more important.

A Useful Decision Framework

If you are choosing a school, ask:

  • Does this program teach electronics?

Then:

  • Does it include hands-on troubleshooting?

Then:

  • Does it cover medical equipment or instrumentation?

Then:

  • Does it teach basic networking?

Then:

  • Do local employers recognize the program?

Then:

  • Can graduates actually get BMET jobs?

Those questions are more useful than:

  • Does the diploma say biomedical?
  • If You Want the Most Direct Path

A solid:

  • Biomedical Equipment Technology

or similar technician-oriented associate degree is hard to beat.

It directly aligns education with the job.

If No Biomed Program Exists Near You

A strong:

  • Electronics Technology

or:

  • Electrical Engineering Technology

program can still provide an excellent foundation.

You can add medical-device experience on the job.

If You Already Have Strong Technical Experience

You may not need another full degree at all.

Look at the employers you want to work for and determine whether your experience satisfies their requirements.

What Did You Actually Prove?

If someone has a degree in biomedical equipment technology, you proved:

They completed that educational program.

You did not prove:

  • They can diagnose every medical device
  • They are a strong troubleshooter
  • They can work independently

Likewise, someone without that exact degree is not automatically unqualified.

Experience, training, reasoning, and technical ability matter too.

Education creates the foundation.

The career is built by what you do with it.

Final Thoughts for Future Biomeds

There is no single degree that defines a biomedical equipment technician.

The most direct route is often a technician-focused program such as:

Biomedical Equipment Technology.

But strong biomeds also come from:

  • Electronics
  • Engineering technology
  • Military training
  • Other technical fields

When comparing programs, ignore the marketing for a moment.

Look at what you will actually learn.

You want a foundation in:

  • Electronics
  • Troubleshooting
  • Test equipment
  • Medical instrumentation
  • Networking

Then get into the field and start applying it.

The diploma can help you get through the door.

What happens after that depends much more on whether you learn to troubleshoot.

And whatever your educational background, keep asking:

  • What did I actually prove?

That is where the real biomed education begins.

— Jake

Important Note

Education and hiring requirements vary by employer, region, job level, and organization. Degree and program names are not standardized, and curricula can differ substantially between schools. Review current job postings and individual program requirements before choosing an educational path, and confirm any specific employer or certification prerequisites directly with the relevant organization.
