Philips V680

Exhalation Valve or Circuit Leak Test Fails

Asset Type

Ventilator

Manufacturer

Philips

Model

V680

What This Guide Helps With

Troubleshoots failed circuit leak or exhalation-valve checks caused by circuit assembly, loose connections, damaged components, water, or accessory leaks.

Step-by-Step Troubleshooting

1. Protect the Patient Before Testing the Circuit

Do not troubleshoot a leak-test or exhalation-valve problem while a patient depends on the ventilator. Move the patient to another verified ventilation source first.

Expected outcome: The patient remains safely ventilated and the suspect ventilator and circuit can be tested independently.

2. Confirm Which Test Is Failing

Determine whether the ventilator fails during a circuit leak check, exhalation-valve check, startup verification, or normal ventilation. Record any displayed message and whether the problem began after a circuit, valve, filter, humidifier, or accessory change.

Expected outcome: The exact failing condition and recent changes are identified.

3. Inspect the Entire Breathing Circuit

Check all inspiratory and expiratory tubing for disconnections, loose fittings, cracks, pinholes, stretched cuffs, damaged connectors, or improperly seated components.

Pay particular attention to connections that were recently disturbed.

Expected outcome: All circuit connections are secure and visibly intact. If reseating a loose connection resolves the test failure, perform the test again and stop troubleshooting after successful verification.

4. Inspect the Exhalation Valve Assembly

Verify that the correct compatible exhalation valve components are installed, correctly oriented, fully seated, clean, and free of visible damage or contamination.

Do not disassemble beyond normal removable user/service components.

Expected outcome: The exhalation valve is correctly installed and moves or seats as designed externally. If correcting installation resolves the problem, repeat the required check.

5. Check Filters, Humidification Components, and Water Traps

Inspect bacterial filters, humidification components, water traps, adapters, and other circuit accessories for cracks, loose seals, excessive water, incorrect installation, or damaged connections.

Expected outcome: All accessories are correctly assembled and leak-free. If an accessory is defective, replace it with a known-good compatible item and repeat the test.

6. Substitute a Known-Good Circuit

When the cause remains unclear, install a known-good compatible breathing circuit and exhalation valve assembly using the approved configuration.

Expected outcome: The ventilator passes its circuit and valve checks with the known-good setup. If it does, the original circuit or valve assembly is the likely cause and troubleshooting can stop after replacing the defective external component.

7. Inspect External Ventilator Ports

Examine accessible inspiratory and expiratory ports for contamination, damaged sealing surfaces, retained debris, or improperly attached adapters.

Expected outcome: Ports and external sealing surfaces are clean, intact, and correctly connected.

8. Repeat the Approved Preoperational Check

Run the appropriate circuit or preoperational verification using the corrected setup and approved test equipment where required.

Expected outcome: The leak and exhalation-valve checks pass consistently. If they pass, complete final functional verification and return to service only when all required checks are satisfactory.

9. Escalate if the Test Still Fails

If a known-good circuit, exhalation valve, filters, and accessories still produce a failure, stop external troubleshooting.

Expected outcome: The ventilator is removed from service for evaluation of possible internal pneumatic, sensing, valve-control, or calibration issues.

If the Problem Persists

Common external circuit, valve, accessory, connection, and assembly causes have been ruled out. A persistent failure may involve an internal pneumatic leak, pressure or flow sensing, valve actuation, calibration, internal tubing, or another service-level condition.

The ventilator should be:

  • Removed from service
  • Labeled Out of Service
  • Sent for repair or bench evaluation
  • Evaluated using appropriate Philips documentation and approved test equipment
  • Repaired or calibrated only by qualified personnel

Complete required circuit, leak, ventilation, alarm, and performance verification after repair before returning the unit to clinical service. Knowing when to stop after a known-good circuit still fails is proper troubleshooting.

Clinical Use Tip

Never use a ventilator clinically after a failed circuit or exhalation-valve check until the cause is corrected and the check passes.

Work Order Documentation (CCR Method)

CCR = Complaint, Cause, Resolution

Complaint

What was reported by the clinical staff.

Example:
"Respiratory Therapy reported the Philips V680 repeatedly failed the circuit leak test during setup."

Cause

What was observed during troubleshooting.

Example:
"Clinical Engineering found a loose connection at an external circuit component that allowed an excessive leak."

Resolution

What action was taken.

Example:
"Connection was reseated, the circuit was reassembled, and the ventilator passed the leak check and final functional testing."

Helpful Details to Include (If Known)

  • Exact failed test or message
  • Circuit type and configuration
  • Exhalation-valve condition
  • Filters installed
  • Humidifier configuration
  • Water or condensation present
  • Loose or damaged connections
  • Known-good circuit result
  • Known-good valve result
  • Final leak-test result
  • Final device status

Final Thought

Circuit and exhalation-valve failures should be approached by carefully checking assembly, connections, accessories, and known-good substitutions before suspecting an internal pneumatic problem. Successful verification is required before return to patient use.

That is successful troubleshooting.
