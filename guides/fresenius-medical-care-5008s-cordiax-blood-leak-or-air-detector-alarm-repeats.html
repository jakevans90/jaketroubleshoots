What This Guide Helps With

Use this guide for repeated blood leak or air detector alarms caused by disposables, bubbles, positioning, contamination, connections, or detector-level faults.

Step-by-Step Troubleshooting

1. Protect the Patient and Treat the Alarm as Real

Do not assume a repeated blood leak or air detector alarm is false. Follow the clinical response appropriate to the alarm and ensure the patient is not dependent on a system whose protective detection function is unreliable.

Move the patient to another verified device when needed before Clinical Engineering troubleshooting.

Expected outcome: Patient safety is maintained and the affected machine can be evaluated without bypassing a protective alarm.

2. Confirm Which Detector Is Alarming

Determine whether the report involves the blood leak detector, air detector, or both. Record the exact displayed message and when the alarm occurs.

Ask whether the alarm began after setup, a disposable change, repositioning, or an actual visible bubble or fluid abnormality.

Expected outcome: The specific detector and operating condition associated with the alarm are identified.

3. Inspect the Extracorporeal Circuit and Disposables

Check the installed tubing and disposable components for correct routing, complete seating, kinks, damaged segments, visible air, loose connections, inappropriate fluid level, or obvious leakage.

Do not manipulate a patient-connected circuit solely for technical troubleshooting.

Expected outcome: The external circuit is correctly installed and free of obvious conditions capable of generating a legitimate alarm.

4. Verify Detector-Specific Positioning

Inspect the tubing or chamber where it passes through the applicable detector. Confirm it is fully seated, correctly positioned, and not twisted, stretched, contaminated, or partially outside the sensing area.

Expected outcome: The disposable is positioned correctly within the detector. If reseating corrects the alarm and it does not recur during verification, troubleshooting can stop.

5. Look for Air or Foam

Check for residual air, foam, or repeated bubble formation in the circuit during a nonpatient test setup. Determine whether an upstream connection, poorly seated disposable, or inadequate priming condition is contributing.

Expected outcome: No persistent air or foam enters the monitored section. If correcting the external cause eliminates the alarm, proceed to final verification.

6. Inspect the Blood Leak Detection Area

For blood leak complaints, inspect accessible detector surfaces and the dialysate path interface for visible contamination or residue according to approved cleaning practices.

Do not open internal optical assemblies or alter detector alignment.

Expected outcome: Accessible sensing surfaces are clean and unobstructed. If approved cleaning resolves the repeated alarm, continue to final verification.

7. Substitute Approved Known-Good Disposables

When appropriate, repeat the test with a verified compatible disposable set to determine whether the problem follows the tubing, chamber, or other external component.

Expected outcome: The alarm either follows an external disposable or remains with the machine. If a known-good disposable eliminates the problem, replace the defective disposable and stop troubleshooting after verification.

8. Verify Protective Alarm Function

After correcting any external cause, perform the applicable approved operational checks to confirm the detector is recognized and the machine operates normally without nuisance alarms.

Do not attempt to defeat, desensitize, or adjust protective detection functions outside authorized procedures.

Expected outcome: No unexplained alarm recurs and the applicable protective function passes required verification.

If the Problem Persists

If circuit routing, disposable condition, air or foam, detector positioning, accessible cleanliness, and known-good substitutions have been ruled out, the problem may involve an internal detector, optical or ultrasonic sensing path, signal-processing, calibration, hydraulic, or control issue.

The device should be:

  • Removed from service.
  • Labeled Out of Service.
  • Sent for repair or bench evaluation.
  • Evaluated with appropriate Fresenius Medical Care documentation and approved test equipment.
  • Repaired or calibrated only by qualified personnel.
  • Fully tested for alarm and protective-system performance before return to service.

Repeated protective alarms with no verified external cause are a valid reason to stop troubleshooting and escalate.

Clinical Use Tip

A blood leak or air alarm is a protective function; never assume it is a nuisance alarm until the complete external circuit and detector interface have been verified.

Work Order Documentation (CCR Method)

CCR = Complaint, Cause, Resolution

Complaint

What was reported by the clinical staff.

Example:
"Dialysis staff reported repeated air detector alarms during setup on the 5008S CorDiax."

Cause

What was observed during troubleshooting.

Example:
"Clinical Engineering found the tubing segment was not fully seated in the air detector and was partially outside the sensing area."

Resolution

What action was taken.

Example:
"The tubing was correctly positioned, a nonpatient functional check was completed without recurrent alarms, and the machine was returned to service."

Helpful Details to Include (If Known)

  • Exact alarm message.
  • Blood leak versus air detector alarm.
  • Stage of setup or treatment when alarm occurs.
  • Tubing and chamber position.
  • Visible air, foam, blood, or contamination.
  • Disposable lot or type if relevant.
  • Known-good disposable substitution.
  • Detector area condition.
  • Alarm behavior after correction.
  • Final device status.

Final Thought

Protective detector alarms demand conservative troubleshooting: verify the patient circuit and disposable interface first, confirm the alarm function afterward, and remove the machine from service when reliable detection cannot be demonstrated.

That is successful troubleshooting.
