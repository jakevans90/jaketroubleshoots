Cooling Fan, Internal Temperature, or Air-Inlet Filter Alarm Persists

What This Guide Helps With

Addresses persistent cooling, temperature, or inlet-air alarms caused by blocked airflow, dirty filters, placement, environmental conditions, or fan-related service problems.

Step-by-Step Troubleshooting

1. Protect the Patient and Remove an Overheating Ventilator From Use

Do not troubleshoot a ventilator with a persistent cooling or internal-temperature condition while a patient depends on it.

Transfer the patient to another verified ventilator or approved backup method. If there is abnormal heat, odor, smoke, fan noise, or unexpected shutdown behavior, disconnect the affected unit from clinical service immediately.

Expected outcome: The patient remains safely ventilated and the affected device is available for controlled evaluation.

2. Confirm the Exact Alarm or Service Condition

Record the complete displayed message and determine whether it references cooling airflow, fan operation, temperature, an inlet filter, or a more general service condition.

Note whether it appears immediately at startup or only after the ventilator has operated for a period of time.

Expected outcome: The condition is reproducible and its timing is documented. If the alarm was transient and does not recur during controlled testing, continue with verification before returning the device to service.

3. Inspect Ventilator Placement and Airflow Clearance

Check that the ventilator is not pushed against a wall, curtain, bedding, equipment cart component, plastic cover, or other obstruction that could restrict intake or exhaust airflow.

Verify nothing has been placed over external ventilation openings.

Expected outcome: All accessible air inlets and exhaust paths have adequate unrestricted airflow. If repositioning eliminates the alarm and the condition does not return during testing, continue to final verification.

4. Inspect the Accessible Air-Inlet Filter

Inspect the user-accessible or maintenance-accessible air-inlet filter according to approved procedures. Look for excessive dust, lint, moisture, discoloration, deformation, or incorrect installation.

Replace a dirty or damaged filter only with the appropriate approved replacement.

Do not defeat the filter or operate the ventilator with required filtration intentionally removed.

Expected outcome: The inlet filter is clean, dry, correctly installed, and not visibly restricting airflow. If filter replacement resolves the alarm, verify cooling performance before returning the device to service.

5. Check the Environment

Determine whether the ventilator is being operated near a heater, direct sunlight, unusually warm equipment, restricted equipment alcove, heavy dust source, or other condition that could increase temperature or restrict cooling.

Compare behavior in a normal controlled environment when practical.

Expected outcome: The ventilator operates in a suitable environment without external heat loading or airflow obstruction. If the condition disappears after correcting the environment, continue with functional verification.

6. Listen for Normal Cooling-Fan Operation

During controlled operation, listen for fan operation and inspect for abnormal fan noise, intermittent starting, scraping, vibration, or complete absence of expected airflow.

Do not insert tools or fingers into ventilation openings.

Expected outcome: Cooling airflow appears steady and no abnormal fan noise or vibration is present. Missing or unstable fan operation requires removal from service and escalation.

7. Inspect External Openings for Contamination or Damage

With the ventilator powered down as appropriate, inspect accessible cooling openings for accumulated lint, dust, foreign material, or physical damage.

Clean only the externally accessible areas using approved methods. Do not perform deep disassembly solely to clean an internal fan or heat sink.

Expected outcome: Accessible cooling openings are clean and unobstructed. If cleaning corrects the condition, continue with controlled operational testing.

8. Perform an Extended Functional Check

Operate the ventilator on a test lung or appropriate simulator under normal test conditions long enough to determine whether the cooling or temperature condition returns.

Observe for normal startup, stable operation, appropriate alarms, unexpected temperature warnings, unusual odor, fan noise, or shutdown.

Expected outcome: The ventilator operates normally without recurrence of the cooling or temperature condition. If the alarm remains absent and required tests pass, troubleshooting can stop.

9. Escalate Persistent Cooling or Temperature Conditions

If placement, filter condition, airflow openings, environment, and obvious external obstructions have been ruled out but the condition persists, stop external troubleshooting.

Possible service-level categories include fan failure, internal contamination, temperature sensing, power-supply cooling, internal airflow restriction, or control electronics.

Expected outcome: The ventilator is removed from service and routed for qualified repair rather than returned to clinical use.

If the Problem Persists

Common external airflow and environmental causes have been ruled out. Remaining causes may involve the internal cooling fan, temperature sensing, internal airflow path, power electronics, or another service-level condition.

The ventilator should be:

  • Removed from service.
  • Labeled Out of Service.
  • Sent for repair or bench evaluation.
  • Evaluated using appropriate manufacturer documentation and approved test equipment.
  • Repaired or configured only by qualified personnel.

Return the unit to service only after the cooling condition has been corrected and required operational, alarm, and extended functional testing has been completed. Knowing when to stop external troubleshooting is proper troubleshooting.

Clinical Use Tip

Never ignore a persistent cooling or temperature warning simply because ventilation still appears normal; thermal problems can progress to unreliable operation or shutdown.

Work Order Documentation (CCR Method)

CCR = Complaint, Cause, Resolution

Complaint

What was reported by the clinical staff.

Example:
"Respiratory Therapy reported that the Vyaire VELA repeatedly displayed a cooling-airflow warning during operation."

Cause

What was observed during troubleshooting.

Example:
"Clinical Engineering found the external air-inlet filter heavily loaded with dust and restricting cooling airflow."

Resolution

What action was taken.

Example:
"The approved inlet filter was replaced, the cooling openings were inspected, and extended functional testing showed normal operation with no recurrence of the warning."

Helpful Details to Include (If Known)

  • Exact alarm or service message.
  • Whether the condition occurred at startup or after warm-up.
  • Ventilator location and surrounding clearance.
  • Air-inlet filter condition.
  • Cooling opening condition.
  • Fan sound and airflow observations.
  • Environmental temperature or nearby heat sources.
  • Presence of unusual heat, odor, vibration, or noise.
  • Results after filter replacement or repositioning.
  • Duration and result of final functional testing.
  • Final device status.

Final Thought

Cooling problems should be approached from the outside first: placement, ventilation openings, filters, and environment can create symptoms that resemble internal failure. Protect the patient, verify each simple cause, stop when service-level cooling problems are suspected, and document the final operational test clearly.

That is successful troubleshooting.
